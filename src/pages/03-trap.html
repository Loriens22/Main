The Logarithmic Trap

Almost every medical intervention ever devised reduces your hazard of death by some multiplicative factor. Here is what that is worth, exactly, in years — and why the answer is so much smaller than intuition suggests.

The derivation

Suppose a treatment divides the age-dependent hazard by a constant factor k at every age. Write the treated hazard and factor the constant into the exponent:

Step 1 — absorb the constant into the exponent μ′(t) = (A/k)·eαt = A·eαt − ln k = A·eα(t − Δ),    where   Δ = ln k / α

The treated population's hazard at age t is identical to the untreated hazard at age t − Δ. A multiplicative hazard reduction is not a different kind of aging. It is the same aging, started later. And because α = ln2 / MRDT, that shift has an exquisitely simple form:

Step 2 — the exchange rate Δ = MRDT · log2(k)   ⟹   one MRDT — about 8 years — per halving of mortality.
The Logarithmic Trap

Benefit scales with the logarithm of effort. Halving all-cause mortality at every age buys ~8 years. Halving it again buys another 8. A hundred-fold reduction — far beyond anything medicine has ever achieved for any population — buys ~53 years. To push the median human lifespan to 150 you would need to suppress hazard at every age by a factor of roughly 105. There is no plausible stack of disease-specific therapies that reaches this. The exponent eats them.

The test: does it reproduce the demographic literature?

A model this simple deserves suspicion. So we check it against a result derived by completely different means — cause-deleted life tables, which ask what happens to life expectancy if a whole category of death is abolished. If a cause accounts for a fraction f of hazard at all ages, then k = 1/(1−f) and:

The competing-risks principle, in one observation

Cancer was responsible for more years of life lost in the United States in 2000 than in 1970 — despite falling cancer mortality rates — because more people survived heart disease long enough to get it. Remove one cause and you hand the survivors to the next. This is why "cancer + heart disease" yields about 8 years rather than 3 + 4 = 7, and why no achievable stack of disease-specific cures reaches radical life extension.

The agreement is not a coincidence and it is not a fit — the model has no free parameters beyond α, which came from mortality data and not from these outcomes. The reason curing an entire disease category buys only a few years is not that oncology or cardiology are ineffective. It is the Gompertz exponent. Remove one cause and the survivors are handed straight to the others, whose hazard is already doubling every eight years.

Where this model is exact, and where it is only approximate

Exact: the identity Δ = ln k/α, for a pure Gompertz hazard with a constant multiplier. This is algebra, not empirics.
Approximate: (i) real hazards include a Makeham term that does not shift, so realised gains are smaller than Δ; (ii) cause-specific shares vary with age, so cause-deletion is only first-order; (iii) human hazard decelerates above ~95, which slightly favours the intervention at extreme ages; (iv) population heterogeneity in frailty biases naive Gompertz fits — see §9. None of these rescue the conclusion. All are second-order against a factor that must reach 105.
