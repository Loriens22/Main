Why aging exists at all

Aging is not required by physics, and it is not a program. It is what happens when natural selection stops paying attention — and that framing determines what can and cannot be done about it.

The selection shadow

Hamilton's central result is that the strength of selection against a deleterious effect declines with the age at which that effect is expressed, because fewer individuals survive to express it and because the reproductive value remaining at that age is smaller. Past the end of reproduction, selection is nearly blind. A mutation that kills you at 80 is almost invisible to evolution; the same mutation acting at 15 is removed immediately.

Two mechanisms follow. Antagonistic pleiotropy: genes that help early and harm late are actively favoured. Disposable soma: because resources are finite and extrinsic death is certain anyway, the optimal allocation to somatic repair is less than perfect. Both predict that aging is a byproduct, not an adaptation — which is good news, because byproducts have no evolutionary defenders. There is no machinery whose purpose is to kill you and which would fight back.

The counter-case, stated fairly

Programmed-aging arguments — phenoptosis and similar — are not simply wrong; they are motivated by a real observation. A 2014 survey of demographic trajectories across the tree of life found species whose mortality is flat with age and species whose mortality declines with age. If aging were a universal consequence of physics, those species could not exist.

The resolution is that this shows aging is optional rather than programmed. Selection can sustain negligible senescence where the ecology rewards it — typically where extrinsic mortality is very low, as in a protected underground colony. That is a far weaker claim than "a death program exists," and a far stronger claim than "aging is inevitable."

Aging is not entropy — and the sloppy version of that argument matters

The claim that the second law makes aging inevitable is wrong, and it is worth being precise about why, because it is the single most common piece of pseudo-physics in this field. Living organisms are open systems far from thermodynamic equilibrium; they export entropy to their surroundings continuously. If the second law forced senescence, the germline could not have persisted for roughly four billion years without aging — but it has, and every one of your ancestors was, at the cellular level, a continuously maintained lineage.

Where an entropic argument does bite is narrower and more interesting: not in energy but in information. The germline is copy-protected by selection and by aggressive repair; the soma is not. Somatic damage accumulates because nothing selects against it, not because thermodynamics demands it.

The strongest quantitative result in the field

Whole-genome sequencing of intestinal crypts across 16 mammal species found that the somatic mutation rate per year scales inversely with lifespan — and that species reach roughly similar somatic mutation burdens by the end of life, despite lifespans differing by more than an order of magnitude. A mouse accumulates its lifetime allocation in four years; a human takes eighty.

This is the cleanest evidence that there is a rate parameter under biological control, that it differs enormously between species built from identical biochemistry, and that it tracks lifespan rather than body size or metabolic rate.

What this licenses, precisely

It does not show that α can be driven to zero in humans. It shows something narrower and more useful: α is a species-specific parameter that evolution has set anywhere from about 2.3 yr⁻¹ in the mouse to indistinguishable from zero in the naked mole-rat, within the same phylum, using the same biochemistry, at the same body temperature.

That is an existence proof, not a route map. But an existence proof is exactly what separates "this problem is hard" from "this problem is forbidden" — and it is the reason the Logarithmic Trap is an argument for changing target rather than an argument for giving up.

What theory has got wrong

  • The rate-of-living hypothesis — that lifespan is set by cumulative metabolic expenditure — does not survive comparative data. Birds and bats live far longer than their metabolic rates predict.
  • The free-radical / oxidative damage theory is largely falsified in its strong form. Antioxidant supplementation trials in humans range from null to harmful, and several mouse models with elevated oxidative stress live normally or longer. The theory dominated the field for decades and generated a supplement industry that outlived the evidence.
  • Longevity escape velocity — the argument that therapies will add life faster than time passes — is coherent as arithmetic but requires that interventions be rate changes. Applied to offsets, it fails immediately: the Logarithmic Trap means each successive decade costs a further ~2.3-fold hazard reduction, forever. Escape velocity cannot be reached by stacking offsets, and the argument has generally not distinguished the two.
