Synthesis · the lead contribution

The framework: two barriers, one target

Everything in this document converges on a single structural claim. Human lifespan is bounded by two independent barriers, both of which are invisible to the statistic the field uses, and both of which respond to only one of the two things an intervention can do.

Barrier one — the Logarithmic Trap

Any intervention that multiplies hazard by a constant is an age-offset of Δ = ln k/α: one mortality-rate doubling time per halving, forever. Benefit is logarithmic in effort. Curing an entire disease category yields single-digit years, and the arithmetic reproduces the published cause-deleted life tables without a free parameter.

Barrier two — the resilience wall

Recovery rate declines with age and the organism's autocorrelation time diverges at some critical age. Offset interventions cannot pass this wall at any value of k. Lowering hazard moves you along the survival curve; it does not move the age at which the dynamical system stops being able to return to its basin.

The convergence

These two barriers were derived from unrelated data — one from mortality statistics, one from blood-marker dynamics — and they impose the same requirement. Offsets are both expensive and bounded. Only a change in the rate parameter escapes either.

And the rate parameter is not fixed by physics. The naked mole-rat is an existence proof that α can be set to approximately zero in a mammal using ordinary biochemistry at ordinary body temperature. What we lack is not permission. It is a measurement.

The proposal, in three parts

Part 1 · The Rate Identification Protocol — report λ and Δ, not "+X%"

Every lifespan experiment should report the two-parameter decomposition of §4 instead of, or alongside, a median percentage:

qtreated(p) = λ·qcontrol(p) + Δ   ·   SD = π/(α√6)   ·   ρ = SD/mean

Three statistics, all non-parametric, none requiring a Gompertz fit, all computable from data that experiments already collect. Detecting the SD difference between a rate and an offset intervention of equal median effect needs roughly 150 animals per arm — well within the scale of a routine ITP-grade study. The discriminating statistic has been computable from data the field has been collecting for thirty years, and it is essentially never reported.

Part 2 · τ-slope — a rate readout that works inside a human lifetime

The obstacle to rate research in humans is that measuring a change in α requires decades and deaths. Critical slowing down offers a way around this, because resilience is a rate, and rates can be measured without waiting for the outcome. The recovery time constant τ is estimable from serial physiology — blood counts, wearable-derived heart-rate and activity recovery — over weeks, within a single individual.

The criterion, stated so it can fail

A genuine rate intervention must reduce dτ/d(age) — the slope of the resilience decline — and not merely lower τ at a point.

A drug that improves τ once and then lets it resume its previous trajectory is an offset in disguise, and it will be trapped in the logarithm no matter how good the initial readout looks. This distinction is invisible to every biomarker endpoint currently in use, because they all measure state at a timepoint.

This converts an intergenerational question into a within-subject one. It also has a property no clock has: it is dimensionally a rate, so a change in it is a change in the quantity of interest rather than a proxy for it — which is precisely what Prentice criterion 4 demands and what state-based clocks cannot supply.

Part 3 · The discriminating experiment

The single highest-value experiment the field is not running, stated concretely:

  1. Take the interventions with the strongest mammalian lifespan evidence — rapamycin, acarbose, 17α-estradiol, caloric restriction — in UM-HET3 mice, both sexes, ≥150 per arm per sex, multi-site, pre-registered.
  2. Report λ, Δ, SD and ρ for each. Predicted result: λ ≈ 1 and Δ > 0 for the pharmacological arms — offsets — with caloric restriction the one plausible exception and the reason to include it.
  3. In parallel, measure τ(age) longitudinally in the same animals from serial physiology. Predicted result: no pharmacological arm reduces dτ/d(age).
  4. Anchor the whole thing against a naked mole-rat cohort measured identically. This provides the positive control the field has never had — an organism where λ is known to be near 1 in the relevant sense, against which the assay's sensitivity can be calibrated.
How to prove this framework wrong

The framework makes claims that can fail, and it should be judged on them:

  • If senolytics or NAD⁺ precursors show λ > 1 with Δ ≈ 0 in a properly powered Q–Q analysis, they are rate interventions and the classification here is wrong.
  • If an intervention increases the SD of age at death without increasing the mean, the α-only interpretation of SD is wrong.
  • If τ and the Gompertz slope turn out to be uncoupled — if an intervention flattens dτ/d(age) while leaving α untouched, or the reverse — then the two barriers are not two views of one parameter, and Part 2 collapses.
  • If rodent caloric restriction is definitively shown to be a slope effect, then a rate intervention already exists in mammals, and the correct conclusion is far more optimistic than the one drawn here.

What is new here, stated honestly

Intellectual honesty requires separating synthesis from invention, so:

Not new

The Gompertz shift equivalence is standard demography. Temporal scaling of lifespan distributions was demonstrated in C. elegans in 2016. The SD-depends-only-on-α property follows directly from the Gumbel form. Critical slowing down in physiology, the Gompertz identifiability problem, and the surrogacy gap in clocks are all published results, cited here.

The contribution

Framing the shift equivalence as a universal exchange rate that bounds every offset intervention; using it to unify the cause-deleted puzzle with geroscience results; the two-parameter Q–Q test as a routine discriminator and reporting standard; the composition of the two barriers into one map; the dτ/d(age) criterion as a within-subject rate endpoint; and the arithmetic in §10 showing the two published routes to the resilience limit disagree.

None of this makes anyone live longer by itself. It is a proposal about what to measure. But the field's central quantity — the rate of aging — is currently estimated by instruments that provably cannot identify it, and no amount of additional biology fixes a measurement problem. That seems worth fixing first.
