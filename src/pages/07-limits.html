Limits, plateaus, and a data-quality scandal

Before asking whether human lifespan has a ceiling, it is worth asking whether the records at the top of the distribution are real. A substantial fraction of them are not.

Is there a hard limit?

In 2016 a Nature paper argued that maximum human lifespan had plateaued around 115 years and that the record of 122 was an outlier unlikely to be exceeded. It drew five published rebuttals in the same journal within a year, largely targeting its segmented-regression analysis: the breakpoint was chosen post hoc, the apparent plateau was sensitive to that choice, and the analysis discarded information by using only the single oldest death per year. Later Bayesian and extreme-value analyses have generally failed to find evidence of a hard cap within the observable range.

Against that, the 2024 Nature Aging analysis of the eight longest-lived populations plus Hong Kong and the US found that life-expectancy improvement has been decelerating since 1990, with mortality compression and declining lifespan inequality — and concluded that survival to 100 is unlikely to exceed roughly 15% of women and 5% of men. Radical life extension this century, on current trajectories, is implausible unless the biology of aging itself is slowed. That caveat is doing enormous work, and it is exactly the offset-versus-rate distinction restated in demographic language.

The records themselves are contaminated

The Blue Zones do not survive auditing

The designated Blue Zones of Sardinia, Okinawa and Ikaria correspond to regions with low incomes, low literacy, high crime rates and short life expectancy relative to their national averages — precisely the profile that predicts poor vital registration.

Okinawa is the sharpest case: the prefecture has the highest per-capita murder rate, the worst over-65 dependency ratio, the second-lowest median income, and the lowest median lifespan of all 47 Japanese prefectures — while being marketed worldwide as a longevity capital. Both claims cannot be true in the way the popular literature asserts.

In fairness to the other side: defenders argue that Okinawa's older cohorts were genuinely exceptional and its younger cohorts have deteriorated on a Westernised diet. That is a real and partially valid argument — but it is a different claim from the one the Blue Zones brand makes, and testing it requires cohort-specific validated records, which for Okinawa are systematically weak because pre-war family registers were substantially destroyed in 1945.

The birthdate distribution of supercentenarians is concentrated on days divisible by five. Age heaping on multiples of five and ten is the standard forensic marker of age misreporting in historical demography, measured by the Whipple and Myers indices. Finding it in the recorded birth dates of supposedly document-validated supercentenarians is close to dispositive of widespread fabrication within that subset.

Why this matters for the science, not just the trivia

  • Late-life mortality plateaus may be artifacts. If the oldest ages contain a mixture of real and mis-recorded individuals, the apparent flattening of hazard after ~105 is partly what you would expect from measurement error alone. The plateau is a load-bearing fact in several theoretical models — including reliability theory — so its status is not a side issue.
  • The 122-year record has a live validation dispute. It has been challenged and defended; the defences are, on balance, stronger. But a field that treats a single contested data point as the anchor of its maximum-lifespan estimate is doing something methodologically fragile.
  • Dietary and lifestyle inferences drawn from Blue Zones are built on the contaminated stratum. Nutrition advice has been derived, at scale, from populations whose extreme-age claims correlate with poor record-keeping rather than with longevity.
The improvement this suggests

Extreme-age validation should carry a machine-checkable provenance standard: contemporaneous document required, independence of the pension incentive demonstrated, and an automatic age-heaping test (Whipple/Myers) reported for every cohort before it enters any dataset used for lifespan-limit inference. None of this is technically hard. It is simply not currently required — and any analysis of maximum human lifespan that does not report it should be treated as provisional.
