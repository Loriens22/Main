The Rate Problem

A quantitative audit of what is actually proven about extending human life — and a formal argument that the field has spent forty years optimising the wrong parameter.

Figure 0 · live simulation A synthetic cohort of 4,000 humans, aged under the Gompertz–Makeham law
Each dot is one simulated person, drifting rightward through age. Dots extinguish stochastically at the empirical age-specific hazard. No individual is scheduled to die at a particular age — yet the population traces the same smooth survival curve every run. That reproducibility is the central fact of this document: aging is not a schedule, it is a rate, and a rate is a thing you can in principle change.
What we found

Essentially every intervention with credible human evidence — statins, blood-pressure control, exercise, smoking cessation, GLP-1 agonists, senolytics — acts as a multiplicative reduction in hazard. Under a Gompertz law such interventions are mathematically equivalent to a fixed backward shift in age, and their benefit is logarithmic in their strength.

What follows

Halving all-cause mortality at every age buys exactly one mortality-rate doubling time — about eight years — and not one year more. Reaching 150 would require suppressing hazard roughly a hundred-thousand-fold at every age. The ceiling is not thermodynamic. It is that we are pushing on the intercept, not the slope.
