The measurement problem

Aging clocks are the instrument on which the entire modern longevity trial enterprise depends. They have a design flaw at the root, and a validation gap at the top.

The clock paradox

Epigenetic clocks are built by regressing DNA methylation on chronological age. The training objective is to predict how many birthdays a person has had. But the quantity anyone actually cares about — biological age — is by construction the part the clock fails to predict: the residual.

The paradox, stated exactly

A clock's usefulness as a biological measure lives entirely in its error against chronological age. So the better a clock gets at its training objective, the less room remains for it to carry biological information. A hypothetical perfect clock — r = 1.0 with chronological age — would be biologically worthless, because it would tell you nothing you could not read off a birth certificate.

The field routinely advertises clocks by their correlation with chronological age. That number is, past a point, a measure of how little the clock can possibly tell you.

The validation gap

Whatever their design, clocks could still earn their place as trial endpoints — if they were validated surrogates. They are not. No epigenetic clock has been qualified as a surrogate endpoint by any regulator. Being responsive to an intervention is not the same as being a surrogate for its outcome, and the gap between the two is where most of this field's optimism lives.

The formal standard is the Prentice criteria, and it is worth stating plainly because almost nothing in geroscience meets it:

  1. The intervention must have a significant effect on the true endpoint (here: mortality or a hard clinical outcome).
  2. The intervention must have a significant effect on the surrogate.
  3. The surrogate must be prognostic for the true endpoint.
  4. The effect of the intervention on the true endpoint must be fully captured by the surrogate — conditional on the surrogate, treatment adds no further information.

Criterion 4 is the hard one and it is the one that matters. A clock could be perfectly prognostic and perfectly responsive, and still be an invalid surrogate — if the drug moves the clock by a route that does not touch mortality. Given that clocks are trained on methylation patterns whose causal relationship to death is largely unknown, this is not a hypothetical concern.

A worked example of the gap

The most-cited human "epigenetic age reversal" result — a claimed reversal of about 2.5 years alongside thymic regeneration — came from a study with nine participants and no control group. It is cited as evidence that human aging can be reversed. It is evidence that nine people's clock readings moved, in an uncontrolled setting, on a measure that is not a validated surrogate for anything.

Compare with the largest properly controlled human caloric-restriction trial, which found the pace-of-aging measure slowed by a few percent while two other major clocks did not move at all. When three clocks disagree about the same intervention in the same participants, at most one of them can be measuring the thing they all claim to measure.

The fix

Three changes would put the field on sound footing, and none requires new technology:

  • Train on time-to-death, not on chronological age. If the target is mortality, regress on mortality. Second-generation clocks partially do this, and they are measurably better prognostically — the lesson has not been generalised.
  • Report technical reliability (ICC) alongside every clock result, and require that any claimed intervention effect exceed the test–retest noise floor. This is routine in clinical chemistry and essentially absent here.
  • Stop reporting correlation with chronological age as a quality metric. It rewards exactly the wrong optimisation. Report prognostic performance for hard endpoints instead, and formally test criterion 4 before any clock is used as a trial endpoint.
To be fair to the clocks

Second-generation clocks trained on mortality-linked phenotypes are genuinely prognostic for mortality at the population level, and pace-of-aging measures are conceptually much closer to the rate quantity this document argues for — they estimate a slope rather than a state, which is exactly the right instinct. The criticism here is not that clocks are useless. It is that they are being used as a decision-grade endpoint before anyone has demonstrated they can bear that weight.
