Offset or rate? Three ways to tell

If offsets are trapped in a logarithm and rate changes are not, then the single most valuable thing an experiment can report is which one it produced. Almost no experiment reports it. Here are three tests that require no new data — only a different statistic.

Recall the two possibilities. An offset divides hazard by k: the survival curve translates rigidly to the right by Δ = ln k/α. A rate change divides the Gompertz slope by s: the entire time axis dilates. In terms of lifespan T:

The two hypotheses, as transformations of lifespan offset:   T′ = T + Δ     versus     rate:   T′ = λ · T

Reported as "median lifespan +12%", these are indistinguishable — which is exactly why the field's default statistic is the wrong one. But they differ everywhere else in the distribution, and that difference is easy to extract.

Test 1 — The Q–Q slope/intercept decomposition

Plot the quantiles of treated lifespan against the corresponding quantiles of control lifespan, and fit a straight line. Do not fit a Gompertz model — that is precisely the step that fails (§9). Just fit:

The discriminating regression qtreated(p)  =  λ · qcontrol(p)  +  Δ
λ ≈ 1, Δ > 0 → pure offset. Bounded by the Logarithmic Trap.
λ > 1, Δ ≈ 0 → genuine time dilation. Compounds.

Report λ and Δ with confidence intervals and you have said everything that matters, non-parametrically, with no distributional assumption. Stroustrup and colleagues showed that in C. elegans a remarkable range of interventions — insulin/IGF-1 mutants, temperature, oxidative stress, hsf-1, hif-1, daf-16 — collapse onto a single universal curve under pure time rescaling.2 Our proposal is the two-parameter generalisation of that collapse, used as a routine discriminator rather than as a demonstration of universality.

Test 2 — The standard deviation of age at death

The simplest diagnostic in the whole document

For a Gompertz lifespan distribution the standard deviation of age at death is SD = π/(α√6). It depends on α alone. The intercept A cancels completely.

Therefore: any intervention that extends lifespan without increasing the spread of ages at death acted on the intercept. No model fitting, no curve regression — just the standard deviation of a column of numbers the experiment already collected.

Test 3 — The coefficient of variation

Because the mean grows only logarithmically with hazard reduction while the SD is untouched, an offset intervention makes lifespans relatively more uniform — the coefficient of variation ρ = SD/mean falls. A rate intervention dilates both, so ρ rises slightly. The direction of ρ is a free second opinion.

The verdict, stated conservatively

Across mammals, no intervention has been shown, reproducibly and without dispute, to reduce the Gompertz slope. Rodent caloric restriction is the one live candidate and the literature contradicts itself about it. Meanwhile the single best-validated pharmacological intervention in mammals — rapamycin — appears to make the demographic rate of aging worse while delivering its very real benefit entirely through the intercept.

Every one of those benefits is worth having. Rapamycin's +23–26% median lifespan in mice is a genuine and important result. But it is an offset, and offsets live inside the logarithm.

Why so many published "slope" claims cannot be trusted either way

Fitting A and α jointly by least squares is an ill-posed problem: the two parameters are strongly collinear, and the optimiser returns a degenerate manifold of nearly-equally-good solutions rather than a point estimate. This is the origin of the long-debated Strehler–Mildvan correlation, which a 2025 PNAS analysis identifies as substantially a mathematical artifact of slope–intercept fitting rather than a biological law.3 This is the deepest reason to prefer the three tests above: none of them ever fits A and α separately, so none of them can fall into the degeneracy.
