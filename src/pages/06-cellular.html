What is actually going wrong in the cell

The Hallmarks of Aging is the field's organising framework. It is an excellent taxonomy and a weak causal theory, and the difference between those two things explains a great deal of wasted effort.

The 2013 framework named nine hallmarks; the 2023 revision expanded it to twelve. To qualify, a hallmark should manifest with age, accelerate aging when experimentally worsened, and slow aging when experimentally improved. In practice the third criterion — the one that matters — is satisfied for only a minority, and almost always in mice.

Taxonomy is not mechanism

A list of twelve things that go wrong together in an aging organism does not tell you which of them is upstream. Because all twelve co-vary with age, correlational evidence cannot rank them, and the framework offers no way to distinguish a driver from a consequence. Its real function is as a shared vocabulary — which is genuinely useful — not as a map of causation, which is how it is usually deployed.

Cellular senescence — the strongest story, with real caveats

Genetically clearing p16-positive cells extends median lifespan in mice, and senolytic drugs reproduce parts of the phenotype. This is the most convincing mechanistic result in the hallmarks literature. It also carries a problem that undermines a large fraction of the field:

p16INK4a is not senescence-specific

p16 and SA-β-gal are induced in macrophages as part of a reversible physiological immune response, and can be induced in p53-null mice — unlike genuine senescence. Every INK-ATTAC clearance experiment therefore ablates an unknown mixture of senescent cells and activated macrophages, and every human trial reporting "reduced p16⁺ cells" is reporting a composite of the two.

This may be the actual explanation for contradictory results in the literature — including a senolytic trial in which CSF IL-6 and GFAP rose after treatment, exactly what you would expect if you had removed a macrophage population that was restraining inflammation rather than driving it.

The fix: mandate multi-marker senescence scoring, simultaneous myeloid-lineage exclusion (CD68, F4/80, LYZ2, CD14) at single-cell resolution, and an inducible lineage-tracing control that spares p16-expressing myeloid cells. Falsifiable prediction: in a myeloid-sparing INK-ATTAC variant, the lifespan benefit is preserved or increased while the reported liver-fibrosis phenotype is attenuated.

The human evidence is far thinner than its citation pattern suggests. The first-in-human demonstration that senolytics reduce senescent-cell burden was n = 9, open-label, with no control group — and it subsequently required a corrigendum stating that re-analysis of the raw data had changed some of the paper's conclusions. That paper is the load-bearing citation for "senolytics work in humans." The corrigendum is cited essentially never.

Partial reprogramming — the field's biggest hope and its most abused statistic

Cyclic expression of Yamanaka factors restores youthful epigenetic patterns and, in some settings, function. The headline lifespan numbers, however, do not mean what they are usually taken to mean:

  • The famous +33% median lifespan result came from progeroid mice — LmnaG609G homozygotes with a baseline lifespan measured in weeks. Rescuing a monogenic lamin defect is not evidence about the rate of normal aging. Wild-type mice in that same study received injury-regeneration assays, not lifespan measurement.
  • The falsifying experiment has largely been done, and was negative. Long-term partial reprogramming in physiologically aged wild-type mice produced molecular and tissue rejuvenation without demonstrated lifespan extension.
  • "+109% lifespan" meant about +4%. One widely-reported result measured median remaining lifespan from 124 weeks, in animals whose controls lived to ~129 weeks. A 109% increase on ~5 remaining weeks is roughly +5 weeks absolute — a gap of about 25× between the headline and the effect on total lifespan.
A reporting standard that would fix most of this

Percentage changes in "remaining" lifespan must never be stated without the absolute baseline in the same sentence. The correct estimand is the restricted mean survival time difference in days with a 95% CI over a pre-specified horizon — it is interpretable in units of time, does not assume proportional hazards, and cannot be inflated by choosing a late start age.

Telomeres — and the trade-off nobody advertises

Telomerase gene therapy extends lifespan in mice. But human genetic evidence points the other way on the thing people most want it to fix: Mendelian randomisation studies find longer telomeres are causally associated with increased cancer risk. Telomere shortening is partly a tumour-suppression mechanism, and lengthening telomeres trades one age-related failure mode for another. Any intervention that increases cellular renewal capacity inherits this trade-off; it is not specific to telomerase.
