Resilience and the dynamic wall

There is a second ceiling, independent of the Logarithmic Trap, and it comes not from mortality statistics but from the physics of how a body returns to normal after being pushed.

Treat the organism as a dynamical system sitting in a stable basin. Illness, injury and exertion push it out; homeostasis pulls it back. The relevant quantity is the recovery rate — how fast the pull acts. In the language of dynamical systems, a system approaching a bifurcation exhibits critical slowing down: the recovery rate falls toward zero, fluctuations grow, and the autocorrelation time diverges. It is the same signature that precedes tipping points in ecosystems and climate systems.

Longitudinal analysis of blood-count data found exactly this pattern in humans. A composite physiological state indicator shows an autocorrelation time that lengthens with age: people in their forties took roughly two weeks to recover from a perturbation; people in their nineties took over eight weeks. The inverse variance of the indicator declines approximately linearly with age, and extrapolating it to zero — the point at which resilience vanishes entirely and any perturbation becomes unrecoverable — gives a critical age somewhere between 120 and 150 years.

Why this matters more than another lifespan estimate

If a resilience wall at ac is real, then offset interventions are hard-capped by it and cannot pass it at any value of k. Lowering hazard by a hundred-fold moves you along the survival curve; it does not move the age at which the dynamical system loses the ability to return to its basin at all.

The Logarithmic Trap says offsets are expensive. The resilience wall says they are also bounded. Only something that changes the trajectory of λ(a) itself moves the wall.

A tension in the published extrapolation, worth flagging

Two numbers in this literature do not obviously agree with each other, and as far as we can tell the discrepancy has not been addressed.

Our own arithmetic on the source result

Under the strict linear-resilience model that motivates the extrapolation — λ(a) = λ₀(1 − a/ac) — the reported recovery times imply a specific critical age. Setting τ(90)/τ(40) = 8/2 = 4 gives:

(ac − 40)/(ac − 90) = 4  ⟹  ac ≈ 107 years

But the paper's headline figure, obtained by extrapolating the inverse variance to zero, is 120–150 years. These are two routes to the same parameter and they differ by 15–40%. Possible explanations: the quoted recovery times are rounded and approximate ("about two weeks", "over eight weeks"), so the ratio is soft; or λ(a) is not strictly linear in age; or the variance and autocorrelation estimators have different sensitivities to population heterogeneity — in a heterogeneous population, variance inflates for reasons that have nothing to do with individual resilience.

The point is not that the paper is wrong. It is that a headline number derived by extrapolating a linear fit far outside the observed data range should be reported as a range with the estimator's sensitivity shown — and that when two estimators of the same quantity disagree by this much, the disagreement is the finding. Set the ac slider to 107 and to 150 and note how different the resulting picture of human potential is.

This is, incidentally, an instance of exactly the methodological failure this document criticises elsewhere: extrapolating a linear trend to its zero-crossing far outside the range of the data is the same move that produced the contested "limit of 115" claim. We flag it here against a result we find genuinely important, because the standard has to apply symmetrically.

Reliability theory arrives at the same place from a different direction

Model the organism as a system of many partially redundant components, each of which can fail, and which is born with a substantial load of defects already present. Redundancy means early failures are absorbed without consequence; as redundancy is exhausted, failure rate climbs. This model generates the Gompertz law without assuming it, and it also predicts the late-life mortality plateau — once redundancy is gone, the system fails at the constant rate of its last remaining components.

It is a striking convergence: three independent frameworks — evolutionary theory, dynamical systems, and reliability engineering — all produce exponential mortality, and all locate the rate of aging in a parameter that is set by design rather than fixed by physics. Redundancy depth, recovery rate, and the Gompertz slope are three views of the same quantity.
